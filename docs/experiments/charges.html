Charges

Electric charge fills the space around it with an invisible field, perfectly real all the same, and this experiment makes it visible.

At the display

  • Pick a charge from the palette, anywhere from −2Q to +2Q, and pinch to place it. The field around it appears instantly.
  • Drag a charge around and watch every line in the picture rearrange itself to the new layout.
  • Try DIPOLE, one positive and one negative charge together. That pair runs chemistry; it is the field shape of a water molecule, writ large.

What's really happening

Two rules you have felt since childhood: likes repel, opposites attract, and both fade with distance. Double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. That is Coulomb's law. The interesting part is how the force reaches across empty space.

Physics answers with the idea this experiment paints. A charge changes the space around it, creating an electric field. Every arrow you see answers one question: if a tiny positive charge were parked right here, which way would it be shoved, and how hard? Field lines bundle those answers into threads. They pour out of positive charges, dive into negative ones, and never cross.

The charges here stay where you put them, on purpose: left loose they would crash together within moments, and pinning them in place lets you study the thing that actually does the work, the field itself. The same invisible machinery sits behind static cling, lightning, and every signal in every wire around you.

Worth knowing: lightning is this picture at planetary scale. A storm cloud and the ground become a giant pair of charges, and when the field between them reaches about three million volts per meter, the air itself tears apart and conducts.
