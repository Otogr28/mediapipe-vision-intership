Charges

Electric charge fills the space around it with a field. You can't see it, you can't touch it — but it's there, and this experiment draws it.

At the display

  • Pick a charge from the palette (−2Q to +2Q) and pinch to place it. The field around it appears instantly.
  • Drag a charge around and watch every line in the picture re-arrange itself to the new layout.
  • Try DIPOLE — one + and one −, the pair that runs chemistry: it's the field shape of a water molecule, writ large.

What's really happening

Two rules you've felt since childhood — likes repel, opposites attract, and both fade with distance (double the distance, a quarter the force: Coulomb's law). The interesting part is how the force reaches across empty space.

Physics answers with the idea this experiment paints: a charge changes the space around it, creating an electric field. Every arrow you see answers one question: "if I parked a tiny positive charge right here, which way would it be shoved, and how hard?" Field lines bundle those answers into threads — they pour out of positive charges and dive into negative ones, never crossing.

The charges here stay where you put them on purpose. Freed, they'd just crash together and the picture would be over; pinned, you can study the thing that actually does the work — the field. It's the same invisible machinery behind static cling, lightning, and every signal in every wire around you.

Worth knowing: lightning is this picture at planetary scale — a storm cloud and the ground become the two plates of a giant charge pair, and when the field between them reaches about three million volts per meter, the air itself rips apart and conducts.
