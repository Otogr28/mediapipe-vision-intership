Waves

Drop a few pebbles into a pond that isn't there. Where the ripples cross, they add up — or vanish completely.

At the display

  • Pinch anywhere to drop a wave source. Pick LOW, MID or HIGH first to set how fast it wobbles.
  • Place two sources a hand-width apart and look between them: some lanes ripple hard, others stay eerily still. Those still lanes are real, and they have a name — nodes.
  • Mix frequencies. A slow source and a fast one weave a shifting fabric that never quite repeats.

What's really happening

Waves have a superpower called superposition: two waves can occupy the same water at the same time. They pass straight through each other, and while they overlap, the surface simply adds them up — crest on crest doubles the bounce, crest on trough cancels to nothing.

With two steady sources, that adding-up settles into a pattern: along some lines the two sets of ripples always arrive in step (loud lanes), along others always opposite (silent lanes). This interference pattern is the fingerprint of waves — if you see it, waves made it.

And you've met it before. Dead spots in a room's sound, the colors on a soap bubble, a weak wifi corner of the house — all interference. The display computes it by simulating the actual pond: the wave equation is solved on a fine grid, many times per frame, live.

Worth knowing: noise-cancelling headphones are destructive interference on purpose — they listen to the noise coming at you and play its exact opposite, trough against crest, so the sum at your eardrum is close to silence.
