Waves

Drop a few pebbles into a pond that is not there. Where the ripples cross, they add up, or they vanish completely.

At the display

  • Pinch anywhere to drop a wave source. Pick LOW, MID or HIGH first to set how fast it wobbles.
  • Place two sources a hand-width apart and look between them. Some lanes ripple hard while others stay eerily still, and the still ones have a name: nodes.
  • Mix frequencies. A slow source and a fast one weave a shifting fabric that never quite repeats.

What's really happening

Waves have a superpower called superposition: two waves can occupy the same water at the same time. They pass straight through each other, and while they overlap, the surface simply adds them up. Two crests meeting rise twice as high, and a crest arriving in a trough leaves the water flat.

With two steady sources, that adding-up settles into a pattern. Along certain lines the two sets of ripples always arrive in step, so the water there dances hard; between those lines they arrive opposed, and the water barely moves. Physicists treat this interference pattern as the fingerprint of waves, because only waves can produce it.

You have met it before. Dead spots in a room's sound, the colors on a soap bubble and the weak wifi corner of the house are all everyday cases of interference. The display computes it by simulating an actual pond: the wave equation is solved on a fine grid, many times per frame, live.

Worth knowing: noise-cancelling headphones are destructive interference on purpose. They listen to the noise coming toward you and play its exact opposite, trough against crest, so that the sum at your eardrum is close to silence.
