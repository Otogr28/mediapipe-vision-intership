Spacetime

Einstein's picture of gravity: mass doesn't pull — it bends. This is the famous stretched-sheet picture, drawn honestly, to scale.

At the display

  • Place a SUN, then a NEUTRON star, then a HOLE and compare their dents. Same idea, wildly different depths — that difference is the whole story.
  • Pinch ORBITER to send a marble around a mass, and try PRECESS to see its oval orbit slowly swivel — the effect that made Einstein famous.
  • Pinch with both hands at once to grab the whole scene and spin it. 2D and TOP give you other angles.

What's really happening

Newton said masses pull on each other and left "how?" open. Einstein answered: they don't reach across space — they reshape it. Mass curves spacetime, and everything moving through it just follows the straightest path available. Orbits aren't caused by a tug; they're what "straight ahead" looks like in a curved place.

The sheet is the standard way to visualize that, but most versions cheat: they draw every dent as a dramatic funnel. This one doesn't. A star is not a point — it has a surface, so its dent is a smooth bowl that bottoms out. A black hole has no surface at all: its funnel keeps going, steeper and steeper, until at the horizon the sheet runs vertical. What separates them isn't mass but compactness — how much mass per how little size.

And here's the twist worth taking home: far from either object, the bowls are identical. If the Sun were swapped tonight for a black hole of the same mass, Earth's orbit wouldn't change a bit. Black holes don't vacuum up galaxies — up close they're extraordinary, from a distance they're just mass.

Worth knowing: Mercury's orbit swivels 43 arcseconds per century more than Newton could explain — a tiny error that nagged astronomers for decades. Einstein's curved spacetime predicted exactly that number, and he later said the discovery gave him heart palpitations. PRECESS is that effect, exaggerated so you don't need a century.
