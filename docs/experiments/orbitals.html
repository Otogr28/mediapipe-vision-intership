Orbitals

Every body you drop pulls on every other one, all the time. Two bodies make clockwork. Three make chaos.

At the display

  • Pick a body from the palette — star, planet, moon, comet — and pinch in empty space to place it. New bodies join the dance immediately.
  • Try the presets. SOLAR builds a tiny solar system; BINARY sets two stars waltzing; FIGURE 8 stages a famous three-body choreography that took mathematicians centuries to find.
  • Watch a comet's trail. Long, stretched loops that speed up near the star and crawl far away — then one close pass can fling it out of the system for good.

What's really happening

One rule runs this whole scene: every mass pulls on every other, harder when closer, along the line between them. That's Newton's law of universal gravitation — "universal" because it's the same rule for a falling apple, the Moon, and everything you're placing here.

With just two bodies the rule is tame: they trace perfect repeating ellipses, the ones Kepler read out of planet tables four hundred years ago. Add a third body and something remarkable breaks — no formula exists for where everything will be. Tiny differences in the start grow into completely different futures. That's chaos, and it's why the display, like NASA, simulates: it sums the pulls on every body and steps time forward, thousands of small steps per second.

Slingshots aren't just mishaps here. When a light body swings close past a heavy one, it can steal a little of the heavy one's motion and leave faster than it came — the same gravity-assist trick real space probes use to reach the outer planets.

Worth knowing: the FIGURE 8 preset is a real mathematical discovery — three equal masses chasing each other along one shared figure-eight, found by computer in the 1990s. No one has spotted three stars doing it in the wild, but the math says they could.
