General relativity · gravitational lensing

Black hole

The dark disc in the middle isn't drawn on top of the camera image — it's made from it. Every pixel around it is the camera feed, bent.

At the display

  • Stand behind it. Your own image smears into an arc around the disc — you are being gravitationally lensed.
  • Move something colorful behind the hole and watch it appear twice, once on each side. A lens with gravity makes double images.
  • Find the thin bright ring hugging the disc: that's light which circled the hole — sometimes more than once — before escaping to the camera.

What's really happening

A black hole is what's left when so much mass is squeezed into so little space that, inside a boundary called the event horizon, nothing — not even light — moves outward again. The black disc you see is that boundary: a region of the picture from which no light reaches you.

Around it, Einstein's rule takes over: mass bends the space light travels through. Light still takes the straightest path available — but near the hole, "straight" is curved. Rays that would have missed your eye get folded toward it, so you see things that are actually hidden behind the hole, stretched into arcs and rings.

The display isn't faking the look. For every pixel, it traces the light ray backwards past the hole using the same equations (Schwarzschild's solution) astronomers use for real black holes, and asks: where in the camera image did this ray come from?

Worth knowing: the first real picture of a black hole — M87*, photographed in 2019 by a telescope the size of the Earth — shows exactly the kind of bright ring you're making here. That one weighs six and a half billion Suns.
