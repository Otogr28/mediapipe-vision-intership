Schrödinger's cat

In 1935, Erwin Schrödinger described this setup not as a proposal but as a protest: quantum rules, taken seriously, put a cat in two states at once. Here, you get to run it.

At the display

  • Pinch the cat and set it in the chamber. Inside are the 1935 props: a Geiger tube, a spring hammer, and a sealed flask the cat should be worried about.
  • Pinch FIRE. One alpha particle flies at the Geiger tube. Will the tube click? That's not showmanship — it's a genuine 50/50 quantum coin.
  • While the box is closed, look at the cutaway: both stories play at once, ghosted over each other. Then pinch the box to open it — looking is the move that picks one.

What's really happening

Whether one radioactive particle triggers a detector is, as far as physics can tell, genuinely undecided in advance — not merely unknown, like a hidden coin, but not yet settled. Quantum theory describes the particle after firing as being in a superposition: detected and not-detected, with definite odds (the display rolls them honestly).

Schrödinger's trick was to weld that tiny uncertainty to a cat-sized consequence. Geiger tube → hammer → flask → cat: if the particle counts as "both until measured," then so does the whole chain, and the sealed box holds a cat that is alive-and-dead. He offered it as a "ridiculous case" — his point was that the theory owed the world an explanation of when the two possibilities become one fact.

That question — what exactly counts as a measurement, and when does the superposition collapse? — is still debated today, ninety years on. What everyone agrees on: open the box, and you'll find one cat, with the statistics of an honest coin. The tally under the chamber is your growing experimental record.

Worth knowing: the cat was never meant to be real — it was a complaint dressed as an experiment, and Schrödinger reportedly grew to dislike his own creation. The complaint stuck around anyway: "Schrödinger's cat" is now the shorthand physicists use for the exact spot where the quantum world meets ours.
